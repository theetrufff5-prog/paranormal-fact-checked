CASE FILE / WESTERN NORTH ATLANTIC

The Bermuda Triangle

DISAPPEARANCES VERIFIED / PARANORMAL CLAIMS UNPROVEN

Ships and aircraft have been lost in these heavily traveled waters. The evidence does not establish a uniquely dangerous supernatural zone.

Assessment published September 2026

VerifiedReal losses include USS Cyclops in 1918 and the five aircraft of Flight 19 in 1945.
UnresolvedThe exact cause of some individual losses remains unknown because no conclusive wreckage or record was recovered.
UnsupportedNo evidence shows disappearances occur here unusually often or that a paranormal force caused them.

Fact: no official triangle

The “Bermuda Triangle” is a popular name, not a formally defined geographic region. Its boundaries vary by author, and the U.S. Board on Geographic Names does not recognize it as an official name.

NOAA says there is no evidence that mysterious disappearances occur more frequently there than in any other large, heavily traveled part of the ocean. The U.S. Navy and Coast Guard likewise do not identify supernatural forces as an explanation.

Fact: difficult waters

The area commonly drawn between Florida, Bermuda, and Puerto Rico contains major shipping and aviation routes. Tropical storms and hurricanes cross the region, weather can change rapidly, and the powerful Gulf Stream can quickly carry away debris.

Shallow banks, deep water, navigation errors, equipment failures, vessel condition, and human decisions all complicate accidents and searches. These ordinary hazards do not solve every individual case, but they are documented mechanisms—not speculation.

Unknown is not supernatural

When evidence is lost at sea, an investigation may be unable to determine one exact cause. That uncertainty does not establish aliens, Atlantis, a time portal, or another extraordinary explanation.

Flight 19

On December 5, 1945, five U.S. Navy TBM Avenger aircraft departed Fort Lauderdale on a routine navigation training flight. All five aircraft and their 14 crew members were lost. A PBM Mariner sent to search for them also disappeared with 13 aboard.

Naval records preserve fragmentary radio traffic indicating that Flight 19 became geographically disoriented and reported compass trouble. Fuel exhaustion became increasingly likely as worsening weather moved in. No wreckage has conclusively resolved the flight’s final moments, so its exact fate remains unknown.

The search plane’s loss is often folded into one supernatural event, but the Navy’s historical account points to a likely in-flight fire. A ship reported seeing a fireball, radar contact disappeared, and the PBM model was known to be vulnerable to gasoline-vapor fires.

USS Cyclops

USS Cyclops, a Navy collier carrying manganese ore from Brazil, disappeared after leaving Barbados in early March 1918. All 306 people aboard were lost. No confirmed wreck has been found and the cause remains undetermined.

It is the U.S. Navy’s largest noncombat loss of life involving a single ship. Its unresolved ending is historically significant, but the absence of a final answer is not physical evidence of paranormal intervention.

Misrepresented: “without a trace”

Popular lists often flatten very different accidents into identical “vanishings.” The tanker Marine Sulphur Queen, lost in February 1963, is an important example: searchers recovered identifiable debris and personal effects. A Coast Guard investigation also examined the converted vessel’s condition and history.

The board could not determine a single precise cause, but that is not the same as leaving no trace. Removing recovered evidence and known risk factors makes an accident sound more mysterious than the historical record supports.

Unsubstantiated explanations

Stories have blamed magnetic anomalies, Atlantis, extraterrestrials, time warps, sea monsters, and sudden releases of methane gas. None has been demonstrated as a common cause connecting the region’s famous cases.

Compasses can vary from true north, but magnetic variation is a normal, mapped feature of navigation—not proof of a unique paranormal field. A proposed mechanism is not evidence that it occurred in a particular disappearance.

Investigators and television

The legend has been revisited by History’s The Bermuda Triangle: Into Cursed Waters, National Geographic’s Drain the Bermuda Triangle and Atlas of Cursed Places, and episodes of In Search Of. Some productions use divers, sonar, aviation records, and maritime experts; others foreground the curse as entertainment.

A program can document a wreck or test an explanation without proving that every famous disappearance is connected. Claims made in dramatic narration still require support from records, recoveries, and reproducible evidence.

Assessment

The ships, aircraft, and lives lost are real. Some cases remain unresolved, while others have been inaccurately retold, stripped of evidence, or grouped together only after the fact.

Storms, currents, navigation difficulty, mechanical problems, vessel condition, and human error provide established risks. The reviewed record does not show a higher-than-normal disappearance rate or a supernatural cause. The appropriate status is DISAPPEARANCES VERIFIED / PARANORMAL CLAIMS UNPROVEN.
